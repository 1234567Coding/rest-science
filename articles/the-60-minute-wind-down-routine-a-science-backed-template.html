    The 60-Minute Wind-Down Routine: A Science-Backed Template

    By the Rest Science Team Published September 24, 2026 6 min read

    Rest Science may earn a commission on purchases made through some links on this page. It never changes our verdicts. Read our affiliate & advertising disclosure.

    Sleep doesn't start when your head hits the pillow — it starts with the hour before it. This is a complete, timed wind-down protocol: the physiology behind each block, rules that survive real life, and what to do when you're still awake at 2 a.m.

    Why an hour — and why routines beat willpower

    Your brain transitions to sleep through a sequence: declining light signals melatonin release, core body temperature drops, cognitive arousal winds down. None of this happens instantly when you decide "bedtime now." A wind-down routine works because it's a conditioned cue: repeated nightly, the sequence itself starts lowering arousal before you ever get into bed. That's not folk wisdom — it's classical conditioning, the same mechanism behind Pavlov's dogs, applied to your own nervous system.

    Willpower fails at night because decision fatigue is highest exactly when you need restraint most. A routine removes decisions: no negotiating with yourself about "one more episode." The blocks below are fixed; your only job is to start the first one.

    The full 60-minute template, block by block

    T-minus 60: Dim and disarm

    Dim the house lights to their lowest usable setting — or switch to lamps. Bright overhead light is the strongest evening melatonin suppressor in your home, and most people live under far more light than they realize (typical living rooms run 100–300 lux; your brain reads anything above ~50 lux as "still daytime-ish"). Simultaneously: end work and work-adjacent screens, and set out tomorrow's essentials — clothes, bag, keys. Every morning decision you make tonight is one less item in the worry loop that keeps people awake.

    T-minus 45: The warm shower

    Shower warm (not scalding) for 10–15 minutes. This is the temperature trick: heating your skin dilates blood vessels in your hands and feet, which accelerates the subsequent drop in core body temperature — and that drop is the physiological signal for sleep onset. Timed here, the cooling curve bottoms out right around lights-out. Research on passive body heating finds it can shorten sleep-onset time by roughly 10 minutes on average. If you shower in the morning instead, move it here — the sleep benefit outweighs the habit.

    T-minus 30: Digital sunset

    Phone on silent, face-down, ideally in another room or at minimum across the bedroom. This is the hardest block and the most important: evening screen light measurably delays melatonin onset, but the bigger enemy is arousal — arguments, deadlines, outrage content. Switch to low-stimulation input: a physical book, journaling, gentle stretching, calm conversation. A slow, already-seen show is acceptable; a new thriller is not.

    T-minus 15: In bed, relaxation pass

    Get into bed at the same time as often as possible. Then run a 5–10 minute relaxation pass: slow breathing with exhales longer than inhales (a 4-7-8 pattern or simply 4-in, 6-out), progressive muscle relaxation from toes to forehead (tense 5 seconds, release), or a body scan. If your mind races, keep a notepad by the bed: write the thought down, deal with it tomorrow. The note works because your brain stops rehearsing what it trusts is captured.

    Lights out

    Same bedtime within 30 minutes nightly. If you're not asleep after ~20 minutes, see the stimulus-control section below — do not lie there marinating in frustration.

    The temperature trick: timing your shower to your cooling curve

    Worth expanding because people get this wrong. The benefit isn't the warmth — it's the cooling after. A shower 60–90 minutes before bed gives your core temperature time to fall below its starting point by lights-out. A shower 10 minutes before bed is counterproductive: you're warm, your skin is flushed, and you've compressed the cooling window. Same for hot baths — 60–90 minutes out, 10+ minutes of soaking. Consistency matters more than exact water temperature.

    The digital sunset: rules that survive real life

    • Charge the phone outside the bedroom. This single change kills late scrolling, morning doom-grabs, and the phone-as-alarm excuse. A $30 sunrise alarm clock replaces the alarm function — and wakes you with light instead of adrenaline.
    • Two-device rule: if you must have a screen, one screen, and only passive content. No feeds, no inboxes, no comment sections.
    • The 20-minute test: if you've been "just checking one thing" for 20 minutes, you've failed the block. Close it without guilt and go to the book.
    • Partner clause: agree on a shared screen-off time. One person's glowing screen in a dark room suppresses the other's melatonin too — negotiate it like adults.

    If you're still awake after 20 minutes: stimulus control

    This is one of the best-supported behavioral techniques in insomnia treatment, and the rule is simple: the bed is for sleep. If you're awake and frustrated after ~20 minutes, get up, go to a dim room, and do something boring — read a dull book, fold laundry, listen to a calm podcast at low volume. No bright lights, no phone, no "productive" tasks. Return to bed only when genuinely drowsy. Repeat as needed. If your pattern is a mid-night wake-up rather than trouble falling asleep, see Why You Wake Up at 3 a.m. — and What Your Brain Is Actually Doing — the 20-minute rule applies there too, with a protocol tuned for that specific case.

    It feels wrong the first few nights. It's retraining your brain's association: bed = sleep, not bed = tossing and worrying. Most people see improvement within 1–2 weeks. Do not nap to compensate the next day if you can avoid it — protect the sleep pressure for the following night.

    Adapting the routine for partners, kids, and late nights

    • Partner on a different schedule: run the routine in the living room; only the last 15 minutes need the bedroom. A sleep mask and earplugs cover a partner who stays up later.
    • Kids: fold bedtime into theirs — dim the house when they go down, do your shower while they settle. Your wind-down can be 30 minutes if the kids' routine ate the first half; keep the shower and the digital sunset.
    • Late nights out: don't skip the routine, compress it. Fifteen minutes of dim lights + no phone + the breathing pass beats nothing, and it preserves the conditioned cue.

    FAQ

    What if I only have 20 minutes?

    Keep three things: dim the lights, phone out of reach, and the breathing pass in bed. That's the minimum effective routine — arousal reduction plus a cue. Add the shower when you can; it's the highest-impact single block.

    Does the routine work if my bedtime varies?

    Partially. The conditioned-cue effect still helps, but a wandering bedtime fights your circadian rhythm directly. Fix the wake time first (it's the stronger lever), then anchor the routine to it.

    Is reading in bed okay, or does that break stimulus control?

    Reading a physical book in bed is fine for most people — it's low-arousal and drowsiness-inducing. The stimulus-control rule targets frustrated wakefulness, not all in-bed activity. If reading keeps you up for an hour, though, move it to the couch.

    Can I use a sleep meditation app?

    Yes, with one rule: audio only, screen face-down, and no browsing the app for "the right" meditation. Pick one in advance. Decision-making is arousal.
